# Anticoagulation reversal: general approach

General reference only -- not a protocol, not a substitute for the
resident's own judgment, a senior resident/attending, pharmacy, or the
institution's current reversal protocol. Reversal decisions depend on
bleeding severity/location, the specific agent, timing of last dose, renal
function, and whether reversal is for active bleeding versus an urgent
procedure -- all reasons this is a "get senior/pharmacy input now," not a
"look it up and proceed alone," situation for a covering resident.

## First questions, regardless of agent

- What anticoagulant, what dose, and when was the last dose taken --
  timing changes everything, especially for the DOACs (direct oral
  anticoagulants), which have much shorter half-lives than warfarin.
- Is this active, clinically significant bleeding (and how severe/where --
  intracranial bleeding is handled with more urgency than a minor
  mucocutaneous bleed) or reversal ahead of an urgent procedure with no
  active bleeding? The two situations call for different urgency and
  sometimes different agents.
- Renal function (affects DOAC clearance and dosing of several reversal
  agents) and any other medications affecting hemostasis (antiplatelets,
  other anticoagulants, NSAIDs).

## Warfarin

- **Vitamin K** reverses warfarin's effect but works over hours (oral) to
  roughly 12-24 hours even IV, so it is not sufficient alone for
  significant active bleeding requiring rapid reversal.
- **4-factor prothrombin complex concentrate (4F-PCC)** is the commonly
  preferred rapid-reversal agent for major bleeding, working within
  minutes to a few hours, generally given together with vitamin K (PCC's
  effect is temporary; vitamin K sustains it).
- **Fresh frozen plasma (FFP)** is an older alternative, generally
  considered less effective and slower than 4F-PCC when 4F-PCC is
  available, with a larger volume load to consider.
- Reversal urgency and target INR are typically guided by the bleeding
  severity/location and local protocol, not a single universal threshold.

## Direct oral anticoagulants (DOACs)

- **Dabigatran** (a direct thrombin inhibitor): a specific reversal agent,
  **idarucizumab**, is available where stocked and provides rapid,
  targeted reversal for major bleeding or urgent procedures.
- **Factor Xa inhibitors** (apixaban, rivaroxaban, edoxaban):
  **andexanet alfa** is a specific reversal agent where available and
  indicated, though availability, cost, and institution-specific
  criteria for its use vary meaningfully -- many institutions still use
  4F-PCC off-label as a practical alternative when andexanet isn't readily
  available or indicated per local protocol.
- Given the shorter half-lives of DOACs compared to warfarin, time since
  last dose and renal function (which affects clearance, especially for
  dabigatran) are especially important in deciding whether reversal is
  even needed versus simply holding the next dose and monitoring.

## Heparin

- **Unfractionated heparin (UFH):** protamine sulfate reverses it
  rapidly and close to completely; dosing is generally based on the
  estimated amount of heparin still active (accounting for heparin's
  short half-life), not simply the total dose given.
- **Low molecular weight heparin (LMWH, e.g. enoxaparin):** protamine only
  partially reverses LMWH's anti-Xa effect (variably, and less completely
  than for UFH), and the approach depends on timing since the last dose
  and bleeding severity.

## What this means practically for a cross-cover resident

- Before reaching for any reversal agent: confirm the actual agent, dose,
  and timing from the chart (medication list, recent administration
  record) rather than relying on what a sign-out note says was
  "supposed to be" given -- this is another situation where
  `compare_signout_to_chart` (USERS.md UC2) is the right tool, not taking
  the sign-out's own wording as verified fact.
- Reversal-agent availability and institution-specific criteria (especially
  for andexanet alfa) vary enough that "what's the right reversal agent"
  is frequently a pharmacy/hematology question, not something to decide
  from general knowledge alone at the bedside.
